Probing Dark Energy on the Moon

Alfredo Gurrola et al. · arXiv:2603.04841 ↗

theorycosmology
TL;DRA laser detector on the Moon could, in principle, tell whether dark energy clumps together or stays perfectly smooth. This paper forecasts how well such a measurement would work.

Research question

Dark energy might be perfectly smooth (the standard ΛCDM picture) or it might clump a little, depending on how fast pressure waves travel through it (its sound speed). The authors asked whether a lunar laser interferometer, measuring tiny stretches of space at very low frequencies, could measure the parameters that control this.

Data & tools

  • No real data. This is a forecast using mock (simulated) signals.
  • Example detector: LILA, the Lunar Interferometer for Laser Astronomy
  • Target frequencies: 10⁻⁷ to 10⁻³ Hz, which the paper says is largely inaccessible from Earth
  • Assumed universe: Ωm = 0.3, ΩDE = 0.7, w = −1
  • Prior: w within 3% of −1, from Planck 2018 + BAO + Type Ia supernovae

Method

  1. Start from the effective field theory (EFT) of dark energy and focus on its kinetic term, with coefficient M₂⁴ (Eqs. 1–3).
  2. Connect M₂⁴ to the sound speed: cs² = c / (c + 2M₂⁴) (Eq. 2).
  3. Model how gravity's potential changes with scale using a simple transfer function (Eq. 14).
  4. Turn that into the strain signal a detector would see (Eq. 15).
  5. Forecast how precisely the parameters could be measured with a Gaussian likelihood and a Fisher matrix (Eqs. 16–17).

Conclusions

  • Horizon-scale strain could directly probe the kinetic part of dark energy's EFT.
  • Strongly clumping dark energy (cs² ≪ 1) would look clearly different from smooth ΛCDM (cs² = 1).
  • This complements existing tests like the integrated Sachs–Wolfe effect.
  • Whether M₂⁴ turns out large or small hints at what deeper theory sits underneath.

Limitations

  • Uses mock spectra and a Fisher forecast, not real data or a full likelihood analysis.
  • Detector noise is design-agnostic, so no real LILA noise curve is used.
  • Approximates the strain as h ≈ ΔΦ/c² and uses a simplified transfer function.
  • Uses only the simplest (minimal) version of the EFT.
  • Strong degeneracies between M̃₂⁴ and cs² (and c̃) make them hard to separate.

To verify

  • No numerical 1σ / 2σ bounds are stated, only figures. Read values off Figs. 1–3 carefully before quoting.
  • The Jeans scale is written as kJ ~ aH/cs, while the transfer function uses csk/aH. Check that the notation is consistent.
  • Typo in the text: “(δq⁰⁰)²” should be (δg⁰⁰)².

My seminar questions
