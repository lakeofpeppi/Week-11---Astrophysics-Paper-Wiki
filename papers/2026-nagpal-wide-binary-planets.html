Swept by the Galactic Tide: How Galactic Tides and Stellar Encounters Drive the Evolution of Wide Binaries and Their Giant Planets

Vighnesh Nagpal et al. · arXiv:2611.15913 ↗

theoryplanetary
TL;DRThe pull of the whole Milky Way, plus stars passing by, slowly disturbs very wide pairs of stars over billions of years, and that stretches, flips or throws out the giant planets living inside them.

Research question

Many planet-hosting stars have a very distant companion star (a wide binary). Unlike single stars, these pairs get reshaped over billions of years by the Galaxy's tidal pull and by passing stars. The authors asked how this changes the orbits and survival of the giant planets inside them, so that planets around wide binaries and single stars can be compared fairly.

Data & tools

  • N-body simulations with REBOUND (plus REBOUNDx for general relativity) (Sec. 2.1)
  • 2500 simulated systems with both the Galactic tide and stellar encounters (Sec. 5.6)
  • Planets and host stars sampled from a Gaia DR5 mock catalogue: super-Jupiters, median 8.1 Jupiter masses, at 1–6.5 AU (Sec. 2.1)
  • Binary companions at 10³–10⁵ AU, on a Sun-like orbit through the Galaxy, run for 9 Gyr (Sec. 2.1)
  • Control runs with no Galactic tide and/or no stellar encounters (Fig. 19)

Method

  1. Draw realistic stars and giant planets from a Gaia mock catalogue, then add a distant companion star (Sec. 2.1).
  2. Move each system along a Sun-like orbit through a Milky Way model so it feels the Galactic tide (Sec. 2.2, Fig. 1).
  3. Add random flybys of passing field stars (Sec. 2.3, 3.2).
  4. Integrate every system for 9 Gyr and record planet eccentricities, ejections, disruptions and binary break-ups (Sec. 3–5).
  5. Compare with control grids without the Galactic tide or encounters (Fig. 19).

Conclusions

  • Giant planets in wide binaries should be more eccentric than those around single stars, especially in older systems and with heavier companions.
  • Binaries tilted nearly perpendicular to the Galaxy's disk lose their planets most often.
  • Galaxy-disturbed wide binaries steadily produce free-floating planets and up to ~3× more hot Jupiter candidates.
  • These are testable predictions for Gaia DR4 and DR5.

Limitations

  • Only one super-Jupiter per system; multi-planet systems could behave differently (Sec. 6.1).
  • Only Sun-like Galactic orbits are simulated; closer to the Galactic centre the tide is much stronger (Sec. 6.2).
  • Tides are not modelled directly, so hot Jupiter numbers are upper limits (Fig. 19).
  • Partial tidal disruptions are not modelled, hence the conservative vs optimistic estimates (Fig. 19).

To verify

  • Sec. 3.3 says ~40% of binaries are ionized by 9 Gyr, but Sec. 5.6 says the binary is ionized with the planet surviving in 43% of systems (1066/2500). A subset can't be larger than the total, so these may come from different simulation grids. Check before quoting.
  • Median ionization time is 2.6 Gyr in Sec. 3.3 but 2.5 Gyr in Sec. 5.6, probably the same grid issue.
