Euclid DR1 Legacy Science on Ultracool Dwarfs: New URLD candidates in Euclid and SPHEREx

N. Lodieu et al. · arXiv:2611.17519 ↗

observational
TL;DRThe Euclid space telescope's first data release reveals over 1,200 unusually red, dusty brown dwarfs, many of them young, which makes them good stand-ins for studying giant planets.

Research question

Unusually red L dwarfs (URLDs) are failed stars with extra-dusty, cloudy atmospheres. They look like young giant exoplanets but float alone, which makes them easier to study. The authors searched Euclid's first data release for new ones, and checked them with spectra.

Data & tools

  • Euclid DR1 photometry: ~1,900 deg² wide survey and ~50 deg² deep survey (Abstract)
  • Euclid red grism spectra (Abstract, Sec. 2)
  • SPHEREx infrared spectra, kept only if S/N > 3 at 1.1 µm and > 10 at 2.2 µm (Sec. 4.1)
  • Known URLDs from the literature for comparison (Sec. 3.2, Table 2)

Method

  1. Find known URLDs in Euclid DR1 to learn where they sit in colour space (Sec. 3.2).
  2. Select new candidates with colour cuts, e.g. IE − HE ≥ 4.4 mag, plus point-source and quality filters (Sec. 3.3).
  3. Cross-match candidates with SPHEREx and Euclid grism spectra (Sec. 3.3, 4.1).
  4. Integrate each spectrum to get a bolometric flux and magnitude (Sec. 4.2, Eq. 1).
  5. Compare colours, spectral types and gravity features to estimate ages (Sec. 5.2–5.3).

Conclusions

  • Euclid hugely expands the known population of unusually red L dwarfs.
  • Their colours point to thick, sub-micron dust clouds in their atmospheres.
  • Most look intermediate-age, but some are very young and low-gravity.
  • They are free-floating benchmarks for young giant exoplanets.

Limitations

  • Most of the 1,240 objects are photometric candidates; only about 40 are confirmed with spectra (Sec. 6).
  • Bolometric fluxes need model-based extrapolation outside the SPHEREx range (Sec. 4.2).
  • Spectral classifications are preliminary (Abstract).
  • Redness can come from youth, metallicity or cloud geometry, which are hard to separate (Sec. 5.3).

To verify

  • Eq. 1 is labelled Mbol (usually absolute magnitude) but the text calls it the apparent bolometric magnitude (Sec. 4.2).
