JWST/NIRISS-SOSS Spectroscopic Variability and Auroral Modelling of Warm Ultracool Dwarf LSR J1835+3259

Eleanor Pike et al. · arXiv:2611.18246 ↗

observational
TL;DRJWST watched a tiny, fast-spinning star with auroras change brightness as it rotates. No standard atmosphere model fits, but heating from the auroras could explain its strange spectrum.

Research question

Some ultracool dwarfs have auroras like Jupiter's, and these may cause their brightness to vary. The authors used JWST to see how the infrared spectrum of the M8.5 dwarf LSR J1835+3259 changes as it spins, to find out what physically drives the variability.

Data & tools

  • JWST NIRISS/SOSS time-series spectroscopy, ~3.6 h on 2023 July 11, covering one full rotation (Sec. 2.3)
  • GTC/MEGARA and Keck/HIRES optical spectra to track Hα activity (Sec. 2)
  • Archival broadband SED, fitted with VOSA (Sec. 3.4.2)
  • BT-Settl model atmospheres and PICASO for perturbed (heated) atmospheres (Sec. 3.4–3.5)

Method

  1. Check Hα emission to see how active the star was during the JWST visit (Sec. 3.1).
  2. Look for aurora fingerprints (H₃⁺ emission, hydronium absorption) in the average spectrum (Sec. 3.2).
  3. Use principal component analysis to split the variability into independent patterns (Sec. 3.3).
  4. Fit a blend of an M-dwarf photosphere and a cooler blackbody (the Hallinan 2015 model) to the spectrum and SED (Sec. 3.4).
  5. Perturb PICASO models with heating and non-equilibrium chemistry to test a more physical explanation (Sec. 3.5).

Conclusions

  • Hα activity has dropped since 2012, so the star was likely in a quieter state during the JWST visit.
  • Two variability patterns exist: one from FeH near 1 µm, one broadband in the H and K bands.
  • No standard model atmosphere fits the average spectrum; a photosphere + blackbody blend fits better but isn't physically realistic.
  • Auroral heating plus non-equilibrium chemistry can mimic that blend, a more physical explanation.

Limitations

  • Observed during a low-activity state, so auroral effects may be weaker than usual (Sec. 3.1).
  • Covers only one rotation (Sec. 2.3).
  • The blackbody blend needs ~63% surface coverage, which the authors call physically implausible (Sec. 3.5).
  • Hotter model grids (Teff ≥ 2600 K) are needed to apply the perturbation model properly (Sec. 4).

To verify

  • The blackbody temperature is 1510 K in the NIRISS spectrum fit (Fig. 9) but 1800 ± 25 K in the SED fit (Fig. 10). These are two different fits, so say which one when quoting.
