A double debris disc around a white dwarf sculpted by two planetary bodies

Nicola Pietro Gentile Fusillo et al. · arXiv:2610.37773 ↗

observationalplanetary
TL;DRA dead star has two separate rings of gas around it instead of one, and each ring is probably being fed by its own crumbling planetary body.

Research question

Some white dwarfs are surrounded by gas from planetary rubble falling toward them. Every one found so far shows the same simple, single-disc signal. The authors found a white dwarf, WD J1328–1450, whose signal looks doubled, and asked what kind of gas structure (and what kind of rubble source) could produce it.

Data & tools

  • X-Shooter spectrograph on ESO's Very Large Telescope, 10 spectra from 2021 and 2023 (Methods, "X-Shooter observations and data reduction")
  • Gaia parallax and colours to pick the target (Methods)
  • Infrared survey photometry (VHS, WISE) to spot the dust (Methods)
  • REFLEX and MOLECFIT for data reduction; emcee (MCMC) for fitting; MCFOST for the dust model (Methods)

Method

  1. Find the star with Gaia and flag it for extra infrared light (Methods).
  2. Take and clean X-Shooter spectra (Methods).
  3. Measure the star's temperature, mass and the metals in its atmosphere (Methods, "White Dwarf Parameters", "Photospheric Metal Abundances").
  4. Model the Ca II, O I and Mg I emission lines as two flat, rotating rings with an MCMC fit (Methods, "Emission lines and double disc model").
  5. Compare Mg emission between the two rings to test their composition (Methods, "Evidence of differentiated composition").
  6. Model the infrared excess to describe the dust (Methods, "Infrared excess and circumstellar dust").

Conclusions

  • The emission lines have four peaks instead of two, best explained by two separate gas rings.
  • Each ring is probably fed by its own planetary body that is strong enough to survive close to the white dwarf.
  • The outer ring has much less magnesium, so the two rings likely come from different bodies or different layers of one body.
  • Current models of how debris gas forms and stays in place can't easily explain this system.

Limitations

  • The magnesium abundance comes from a single line that may be blended with emission, so it may be underestimated (Methods).
  • The O I and Mg I fits are low signal or degenerate, so the outer ring's edges are loosely constrained (Methods, Table 3).
  • The dust model is not unique: several setups fit the infrared data (Methods).
  • The lopsided shape of the inner ring is not explained (Methods).

To verify

  • The simple thin-disc dust fit gives an inclination of about 0°, but the gas rings are tilted about 77° (Methods vs Table 3). Different models, but the paper doesn't reconcile them.
  • The abstract says the bodies range "from a few km", but the size estimates in the main text start at 45 km.
  • One figure caption appears to name the star "WD J1338–1450" instead of J1328–1450 (Fig. 2). Check in the PDF.

My seminar questions

  1. Could the gap between the two rings be cleared by a third, unseen body instead of two separate feeding bodies?
  2. How long can two rings stay separate before they spread into each other?
  3. Would repeat spectra over years show the rings changing, like the precession seen in other gas discs?
